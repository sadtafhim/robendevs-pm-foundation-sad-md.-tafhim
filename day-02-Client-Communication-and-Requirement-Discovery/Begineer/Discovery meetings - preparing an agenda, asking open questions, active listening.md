
# Discovery Meetings

## 1. What is a Discovery Meeting?

A discovery meeting is an initial meeting between a project team and a client to understand the client's business, goals, problems, expectations, and project requirements.

The purpose is to understand what the client wants to build, why they need it, and what problems they expect the project to solve.

Before starting development, the project team needs to gather enough information to understand the client's needs and plan the project properly.

**Example:**

Imagine a client wants to build an e-commerce website. Before starting development, the project manager should understand:

- What products will the client sell?
- Who are their target customers?
- What features do they need?
- How will customers make payments?
- Does the client need an admin dashboard?
- What is their expected budget and deadline?

This information helps the team understand the project before making decisions or starting development.

### Why Are Discovery Meetings Important?

1. **Understand client needs:** Find out what the client actually wants.
2. **Identify problems:** Understand the challenges the client is trying to solve.
3. **Define project scope:** Identify what is included and excluded from the project.
4. **Set expectations:** Discuss budget, deadlines, deliverables, and limitations.
5. **Avoid misunderstandings:** Make sure both parties have the same understanding.
6. **Build relationships:** Establish trust and create a foundation for good communication.

### 1.1 Preparing a Discovery Meeting Agenda

Before conducting a discovery meeting, the project manager should prepare an agenda.

An agenda is simply a planned list of topics that will be discussed during a meeting.

It helps keep the meeting organized, ensures important topics are covered, and prevents discussions from going off track.

Without an agenda, meetings can become unstructured. People might discuss unrelated topics, forget important questions, or spend too much time discussing one subject.

**What Should a Discovery Meeting Agenda Include?**

**1. Introduction**

Start by introducing everyone involved in the meeting. Briefly explain the purpose of the meeting and what you hope to understand by the end.

**2. Understanding the Client's Business**

Learn about the client's company, industry, customers, and business model.

Questions to ask:

- What does your business do?
- Who are your main customers?
- What products or services do you offer?
- What makes your business different from competitors?

**3. Understanding the Client's Problems**

Find out why the client wants to start the project and what problems they are trying to solve.

Questions to ask:

- What challenges are you currently facing?
- Why do you need this project?
- What problems should the solution address?
- What happens if these problems are not solved?

**4. Discussing Project Requirements**

Understand what the client expects from the final product.

Questions to ask:

- What features do you need?
- What should users be able to do?
- Do you have any reference websites or applications?
- Are there any specific technical requirements?

**5. Discussing Budget and Timeline**

Understand the client's financial and time expectations.

Questions to ask:

- What is your expected budget?
- When would you like the project completed?
- Are there any important deadlines?
- Are there any specific milestones?

**6. Discussing Expectations and Next Steps**

Before ending the meeting, make sure everyone understands what happens next.

Discuss:

- Expected deliverables.
- Responsibilities of both parties.
- Additional information required.
- Next meeting or follow-up activities.

### 1.2 Asking Open-Ended Questions

During a discovery meeting, clients may not always know exactly what they want. Sometimes, they have a general idea but struggle to explain their requirements.

This is where open-ended questions become useful.

Open-ended questions encourage clients to explain their thoughts, experiences, problems, and expectations in detail.

Unlike questions that can be answered with a simple yes or no, open-ended questions encourage discussion and help the project manager understand the bigger picture.

**Why Should You Ask Open-Ended Questions?**

- They encourage clients to share more information.
- They reveal requirements that may not be immediately obvious.
- They help identify hidden problems.
- They reduce assumptions and misunderstandings.
- They make clients feel heard and involved.
- They help project managers make better decisions.

**Types of Open-Ended Questions**

**1. Questions About Business Goals**

These help understand what the client wants to achieve.

Examples:

- What are your main business goals?
- What do you want to improve through this project?
- How do you expect this solution to help your business?

**2. Questions About Problems**

These help identify the client's current challenges.

Examples:

- What difficulties do you face with your current system?
- Which processes take the most time?
- What problems do your customers frequently experience?

**3. Questions About Requirements**

These help identify the features and functionality the client needs.

Examples:

- How should the system work?
- What features would make the product useful?
- What should users be able to do on the platform?

**4. Questions About Expectations**

These help understand what the client considers a successful project.

Examples:

- What would make this project successful for you?
- What results do you expect after launch?
- What are your priorities for the final product?

**Closed-Ended vs Open-Ended Questions**

| Closed-ended questions | Open-ended questions |
|---|---|
| Do you need a website? | What are you trying to achieve with your website? |
| Do you need online payments? | How would you like customers to pay for their orders? |
| Do you have a deadline? | When do you expect the project to be completed, and why? |
| Do you need an admin panel? | How would you like to manage your website? |
| Are you happy with your current system? | What problems do you experience with your current system? |

Remember: Closed-ended questions are not bad. They are useful when confirming specific details. However, open-ended questions are generally more useful when exploring a client's needs.

### 1.3 Active Listening

Asking good questions is only half of a successful discovery meeting. You also need to listen carefully to the client's answers.

Active listening means giving your full attention to the person speaking, understanding their message, and responding appropriately.

It is more than simply hearing what someone says. It involves understanding their concerns, asking relevant follow-up questions, and confirming that you have understood them correctly.

During discovery meetings, active listening helps project managers understand the client's actual needs instead of making assumptions.

**How to Practice Active Listening**

**1. Give Your Full Attention**

Focus on what the client is saying instead of thinking about your next question or getting distracted.

**2. Don't Interrupt**

Allow the client to finish explaining their thoughts before responding.

**3. Ask Follow-Up Questions**

If something is unclear, ask additional questions to understand it better.

Example:

Client: "Our current website is too slow."

Project Manager: "Which pages take the longest to load, and how does this affect your customers?"

**4. Take Notes**

Write down important requirements, problems, decisions, and expectations so you can refer to them later.

**5. Confirm Your Understanding**

Repeat important information in your own words to make sure you understood correctly.

Example:

"So, if I understand correctly, your main priority is improving website speed because customers are leaving before completing their purchases. Is that correct?"

**6. Pay Attention to Concerns**

Sometimes clients may express concerns indirectly. Pay attention to their priorities, frustrations, and expectations instead of focusing only on their technical requirements.

### 1.4 What to Do After a Discovery Meeting

A discovery meeting does not end when everyone leaves the meeting. The information collected needs to be organized, reviewed, and shared.

This ensures that the project team can use the discussion to plan the next stages of the project.

**Steps to Follow After a Discovery Meeting**

**1. Organize Meeting Notes**

Review your notes and organize the information into categories such as:

- Business goals.
- Client problems.
- Project requirements.
- Budget and timeline.
- Expectations.

**2. Prepare Meeting Minutes**

Meeting minutes are written records of the important discussions, decisions, and action items from a meeting.

They help everyone remember what was discussed and what needs to happen next.

**3. Identify Missing Information**

Review the gathered information and identify anything that is still unclear or requires further discussion.

For example, the client may have requested online payments but has not specified which payment methods they want to support.

**4. Share a Meeting Summary**

Send the client a summary of the discussion through email or a project management platform.

This gives the client an opportunity to review the information and correct any misunderstandings.

**5. Define the Next Steps**

Decide what needs to happen after the discovery meeting.

This might include:

- Preparing initial project requirements.
- Creating a project proposal.
- Estimating project costs.
- Preparing a project timeline.
- Scheduling the next meeting.

---

**The main idea:** A discovery meeting is not about convincing a client to start a project. It is about understanding their business, identifying their actual problems, gathering requirements, and making sure both the client and project team have a shared understanding before the work begins.
