# Stakeholders: Identifying Decision Makers, Users, and Approvers

## 1. What Are Stakeholders?

Stakeholders are individuals or groups who are involved in a project, influence its decisions, or are affected by its outcome. In a software project, stakeholders may include the business owner, client project manager, employees, end users, and the development team. They are important because they provide requirements, make decisions, give feedback, and approve project deliverables.

## 2. Why Is Identifying Stakeholders Important?

Not everyone involved in a project has the same responsibilities or authority. Some people make business decisions, others use the software, and some are responsible for approving completed work. If these roles are not identified early, the project may face delayed decisions, conflicting instructions, unclear requirements, unnecessary rework, and approval delays. Identifying stakeholders helps the project manager understand who to communicate with, who makes decisions, and who has the authority to approve the work.

## 3. Three Important Types of Client-Side Stakeholders

### A. Decision Makers

Decision makers are people who have the authority to make important business and project decisions. They determine the project's direction, priorities, budget, and major requirements. They may also decide whether proposed changes are worth the additional time and cost. Common decision makers include business owners, CEOs, directors, and senior managers. A project manager should identify who has the final authority to approve budgets, change project scope, and make major decisions.

### B. Users

Users are the people who will actually use the software or application after it is developed. They understand their daily tasks and the problems they face, making their feedback important during requirement gathering and development. They can help identify the features they need, problems with existing processes, daily workflows, and usability requirements. For example, employees using an inventory system may need an easy way to update stock, while managers may need reports to monitor inventory. Involving users early helps ensure that the final product meets their actual needs.

### C. Approvers

Approvers are people responsible for reviewing and formally accepting project deliverables. They check whether the completed work meets the agreed requirements and expectations. Depending on the project, they may approve designs, requirements, prototypes, completed features, or final releases. A project manager should establish who approves each deliverable, what the approval process involves, and how long it usually takes. This prevents completed work from being delayed because nobody knows who has the authority to approve it.

## 4. Understanding the Difference

Although these roles can overlap, their responsibilities are different:

- **Decision Makers:** Decide what the business needs and what direction the project should take.
- **Users:** Use the final product and provide feedback about their needs.
- **Approvers:** Review and formally accept completed work.

One person can hold multiple roles. For example, an HR manager may use an employee management system while also approving its features.

The important thing is to understand each person's actual responsibilities and authority rather than assuming everyone has the same role.

## 5. How to Identify Client-Side Stakeholders

Stakeholders should be identified during the early stages of a project, particularly during discovery and requirement gathering.

A project manager can do this by following a few steps:

**1. Ask the client who is involved**

Find out who will participate in the project, who will use the final product, who will make decisions, and who will approve deliverables.

**2. Understand their responsibilities**

Determine what each person is responsible for and how they will contribute to the project.

**3. Identify their authority**

Clarify who can approve requirements, authorize additional costs, make scope changes, and provide final approval.

**4. Establish communication channels**

Decide who should be contacted for different matters. For example, business decisions may go to the business owner, while usability feedback should come from end users.

**5. Document the information**

Record the stakeholders, their responsibilities, authority, and communication requirements in a stakeholder register.

## 6. Stakeholder Register

A stakeholder register is a document that records the important people involved in a project, their roles, responsibilities, authority, and communication requirements. It helps the project team understand who to contact for specific matters and prevents confusion about responsibilities. For example, a business owner may handle budget decisions, a marketing manager may approve designs, and employees may provide feedback on how the system works. The register should be updated whenever stakeholder responsibilities or project involvement change.

## 7. Managing Stakeholders Effectively

Identifying stakeholders is only the first step. A project manager must also maintain clear communication and manage their involvement throughout the project.

Some important practices include:

- **Define responsibilities:** Make sure everyone understands their role and authority.
- **Establish a primary contact:** Have a main client representative to coordinate communication and organize feedback.
- **Involve users early:** Include actual users in requirement discussions, prototype reviews, and testing.
- **Establish approval processes:** Clearly define who approves each deliverable and how approval is recorded.
- **Document decisions:** Maintain records of important decisions to avoid disagreements later.
- **Provide regular updates:** Keep relevant stakeholders informed about progress, changes, and potential issues.

## 8. Connection Between Stakeholder Management and RACI

RACI is a responsibility assignment framework used to clarify who is involved in completing project activities.

It helps project managers understand who performs the work, who is accountable for the outcome, who provides input, and who needs updates.

RACI stands for:

- **R – Responsible:** The person who performs the actual work.
- **A – Accountable:** The person who owns the outcome and has final accountability.
- **C – Consulted:** People who provide feedback or expertise.
- **I – Informed:** People who need to receive updates about progress or decisions.

For example, when designing a website, the designer may be responsible for creating the design, the client project manager may coordinate the process, and the marketing manager may be accountable for approving the design.

RACI helps prevent confusion by clearly assigning responsibilities. Approval authority should always be agreed upon rather than assumed from someone's job title.

## 9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Assuming the main client contact is the decision maker:** They may not have final authority.
- **Ignoring end users:** The product may not meet actual user needs.
- **Identifying approvers too late:** Completed work may remain pending.
- **Allowing everyone to change requirements:** Conflicting instructions can lead to unnecessary rework.
- **Failing to document responsibilities:** The team may become confused about who handles what.
- **Involving stakeholders too late:** Important requirements and expectations may be overlooked.
