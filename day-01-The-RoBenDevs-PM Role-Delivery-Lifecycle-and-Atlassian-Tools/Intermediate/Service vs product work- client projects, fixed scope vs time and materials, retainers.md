# Service Work vs. Product Work

## 1. Introduction

Companies in the software and technology industry generally operate through two major types of work: **Service Work** and **Product Work**.

Understanding the difference between these two models is important because they have different business objectives, customer relationships, ownership structures, development processes, and success criteria.

### 1.1 Service Work

Service work is when a company provides its expertise, resources, or technical capabilities to another company or client to solve a specific problem or deliver a particular outcome.

Instead of building a product for its own customers, the company is hired by a client to perform work on the client's behalf.

Common service offerings include:

- Software development
- System implementation
- Maintenance and support
- Technical consulting
- UI/UX design
- IT infrastructure management

**Example:**

A business hires a software development company to build an e-commerce website. The development company gathers requirements, develops the website, and delivers it according to the agreed terms.

This is service work because the company is developing a solution for a specific client.

### 1.2 Product Work

Product work is when a company builds, maintains, and improves a product that it owns or is responsible for, with the intention of serving a market, user base, or specific group of customers over time.

Unlike service work, the company is not simply hired to create a solution for a specific client.

The company has:

- Its own product.
- Its own product vision.
- Its own product strategy.
- Responsibility for long-term product success.

The company decides what to build based on business objectives, customer needs, market opportunities, user feedback, and product strategy.

**Example:**

A company develops its own project management software and offers it to businesses through a monthly subscription.

The company continuously improves the software, introduces new features, fixes bugs, and responds to user feedback.

This is product work because the company owns and develops a product for a broader customer base.

### 1.3 Service Work vs. Product Work

| Area | Service Work | Product Work |
|---|---|---|
| Primary objective | Deliver a solution to a client | Build and grow a product |
| Customer | Usually a specific client or organization | A market, user base, or multiple customers |
| Ownership | Depends on the contract | Usually owned by the product company |
| Requirements | Often defined with the client | Discovered, validated, and prioritized by the product team |
| Scope | Often agreed upon before development | Evolves based on priorities and feedback |
| Revenue | Project fees, hourly billing, retainers, etc. | Subscriptions, licensing, sales, usage fees, etc. |
| Priorities | Client commitments and contractual obligations | Product strategy, user needs, and business goals |
| Success | Delivery, quality, client satisfaction, profitability | Adoption, retention, revenue, user value, and growth |
| Changes | May require approval and additional cost | May be reprioritized within the product roadmap |
| Long-term responsibility | Contractually defined support or maintenance | Continuous product improvement and sustainability |

## 2. Client Projects

### 2.1 What Is a Client Project?

A client project is a project undertaken by a company for another organization, business, or individual who has requested a specific solution.

The client may have:

- An idea for a new system.
- A business problem that needs to be solved.
- An existing system that needs improvement.
- A requirement to implement new technology.
- A need to automate an existing business process.

The service provider is responsible for:

1. Understanding the client's requirements.
2. Planning the project.
3. Assigning the necessary resources.
4. Designing and developing the solution.
5. Testing and implementing the solution.
6. Delivering the agreed work according to the contract.

### 2.2 How a Client Project Begins

A client project generally starts when a potential client approaches a company with a problem, business requirement, or technical need.

Before development begins, the service provider needs to understand the client's business, expectations, and requirements.

The team may conduct:

- Client meetings
- Interviews
- Requirement-gathering sessions
- Workshops
- Technical discussions
- Business process analysis

During these sessions, the team tries to understand:

1. What does the client currently do?
2. What problems are they experiencing?
3. Why do they need the proposed system?
4. Who will use the system?
5. What features and functionalities are required?
6. What limitations exist in the current process?
7. What technical requirements must be fulfilled?
8. What does the client expect to receive?

After gathering the necessary information, the company can:

- Estimate the project.
- Prepare a proposal.
- Define the project scope.
- Determine the required resources.
- Establish the timeline.
- Agree on the commercial model.
- Prepare and finalize the contract.

### 2.3 Client Requirements and Expectations

In a client project, the client's requirements are a major input into the project's scope and planning.

However, clients may not always know exactly what they need.

Sometimes, clients communicate their requirements using vague statements such as:

- "We need a better management system."
- "We want to automate our business."
- "The current system is too slow."
- "We need something similar to our competitor's application."

These statements describe problems or expectations, but they do not necessarily explain the actual solution.

The Project Manager, Business Analyst, or Product Manager must clarify these requirements and translate them into actionable work.

This generally involves:

1. Understanding the actual business problem.
2. Identifying the users and stakeholders.
3. Clarifying functional requirements.
4. Identifying non-functional requirements.
5. Defining system behavior and expected outcomes.
6. Identifying technical limitations and dependencies.
7. Prioritizing requirements.
8. Converting requirements into user stories, tasks, and development milestones.

**Important:** The goal is not simply to build what the client initially asks for. The goal is to understand the problem and deliver a solution that fulfills the client's actual business needs.

### 2.4 Client Communication and Approval

Communication with the client is important throughout every stage of a project.

A client may have multiple stakeholders, including:

- Business owners
- Decision-makers
- Managers
- Technical teams
- Department heads
- End users

Making sure these stakeholders remain informed is essential for project success.

The service provider should maintain regular communication regarding:

- Project progress
- Completed tasks
- Upcoming milestones
- Technical challenges
- Potential risks
- Changes in requirements
- Budget and timeline
- Pending decisions and approvals

Client approval may be required at different stages, such as:

- Requirement approval
- UI/UX design approval
- Development milestone approval
- User Acceptance Testing (UAT)
- Final delivery approval

Clear communication helps prevent misunderstandings, reduces unnecessary rework, and ensures that the delivered solution meets the client's expectations.

## 3. Commercial Models in Service Work

In service-based companies, clients generally pay for development and other services through different commercial models.

Three common arrangements are:

1. Fixed Scope / Fixed Price
2. Time and Materials (T&M)
3. Retainer

Each model has different pricing structures, responsibilities, financial risks, and levels of flexibility.

---

## 4. Fixed Scope / Fixed Price

### 4.1 What Is Fixed Scope / Fixed Price?

A Fixed Scope project is an arrangement where the client and service provider agree on a defined set of deliverables, requirements, and work to be completed before or during contract negotiation.

The project is typically associated with:

- A predetermined price.
- An agreed project scope.
- A defined delivery schedule.
- Specific deliverables.
- Acceptance criteria.

The client knows what they are purchasing, and the service provider agrees to deliver the specified work under the agreed conditions.

**Example:**

A client hires a software company to develop an inventory management system.

The agreed scope includes:

- User authentication
- Product management
- Inventory tracking
- Stock reports
- Admin dashboard

The company agrees to deliver these features within three months for BDT 800,000.

Both parties agree on the scope, price, and delivery expectations before development begins.

This is a Fixed Scope / Fixed Price project.

### 4.2 Advantages of Fixed Scope

**For the Client**

- Knows the expected project cost.
- Has a clearer delivery expectation.
- Can budget more easily.
- Has defined deliverables and acceptance criteria.
- Can measure project completion against agreed requirements.

**For the Service Provider**

- Can plan resources and development activities.
- Can estimate project profitability.
- Can manage milestones and deliverables.
- Can establish a clear agreement with the client.
- Can allocate team members based on the estimated workload.

### 4.3 Disadvantages of Fixed Scope

Fixed Scope projects can become problematic when the requirements are not properly understood before development begins.

Sometimes, clients introduce additional requirements after the project has started.

For example:

The client initially requests an inventory management system with five features. During development, they request an additional reporting module, mobile application support, and advanced analytics.

These additional requirements may increase the workload.

If the service provider accepts all these changes without adjusting the cost, timeline, or resources, the project may suffer from:

- Scope creep
- Missed deadlines
- Increased development costs
- Developer overload
- Reduced quality
- Lower profit margins

This leads to an important project management concept: **Scope Creep**.

---

## 5. Scope Creep and Change Management

### 5.1 What Is Scope Creep?

Scope creep occurs when additional requirements, features, or work are gradually introduced into a project without properly adjusting the scope, budget, timeline, resources, or agreement.

Scope creep is particularly dangerous in Fixed Scope projects because the service provider has already committed to delivering a specific amount of work for an agreed price.

When additional work is continuously introduced without proper adjustments, the original project estimation becomes inaccurate.

**Example:**

A client initially agrees to develop a website with the following features:

- Home page
- About page
- Contact page
- User registration
- Basic admin panel

During development, the client requests:

- Online payment integration
- Advanced analytics
- Multi-language support
- Mobile application integration
- Additional reporting features

These requirements were not included in the original agreement.

If the development team implements everything without adjusting the price or timeline, the project experiences scope creep.

### 5.2 How a Project Manager Handles Scope Creep

The Project Manager should not simply reject every new request or automatically accept everything.

Instead, every additional request should go through a formal change management process.

**Step 1: Client Requests a Change**

The client communicates a new requirement or requests a modification to the existing scope.

**Step 2: Impact Assessment**

The Project Manager and technical team analyze the potential impact of the change.

They consider:

- Additional development effort
- Cost implications
- Timeline impact
- Technical risks
- Dependencies
- Resource availability
- Quality implications

**Step 3: Change Decision**

Based on the assessment, the change can be:

- Approved
- Rejected
- Deferred
- Accepted by replacing an existing requirement

**Step 4: Update the Agreement**

If the change is approved, the project scope, budget, timeline, resources, and deliverables should be updated where necessary.

This may require a formal change request or contract amendment.

**Step 5: Implement the Approved Change**

After approval and agreement, the development team can proceed with implementation.

### Change Management Flow

```text
Client Requests a Change
          |
          v
    Impact Assessment
          |
          v
     Change Decision
          |
          v
   Update the Agreement
          |
          v
 Implement Approved Change
 ```

## 6. Time and Materials (T&M)

### 6.1 What Is Time and Materials?

Time and Materials (T&M) is a commercial model where the client pays for the actual time spent working and the resources or materials consumed, based on agreed rates.

Instead of agreeing that the entire project will cost a fixed amount, the client agrees to pay for the work performed.

The total cost is typically calculated based on:

- Hours worked
- Developer rates
- Designer rates
- QA engineer rates
- Project Manager rates
- Other agreed expenses

**Example:**

A client hires a development company to build a business management application.

The client and service provider agree on the following hourly rates:

| Resource | Hourly Rate |
|---|---:|
| Software Developer | BDT 2,000 |
| UI/UX Designer | BDT 1,500 |
| QA Engineer | BDT 1,200 |
| Project Manager | BDT 1,800 |

At the end of the billing period, the service provider calculates the total payment based on the actual hours worked by each resource.

The final cost depends on the amount of work performed.

### 6.2 Advantages of T&M

**For the Client**

- Requirements can evolve.
- Changes can be introduced more easily.
- They do not need to finalize every detail before work starts.
- They can prioritize new work based on current needs.
- They can potentially stop, pause, or adjust the work according to the agreement.
- They can receive continuous improvements based on feedback.

**For the Service Provider**

- Less risk of underestimating a large and uncertain scope.
- Additional work can be billed.
- The team can adjust to new requirements.
- The contract can accommodate changing priorities.
- There is less pressure to absorb unplanned work within a fixed project price.

### 6.3 Disadvantages of T&M

**For the Client**

- Total cost is less predictable.
- The project may take longer than expected.
- Requires trust in the service provider.
- Requires careful budget monitoring.
- Continuous changes may increase overall expenditure.

**For the Service Provider**

- Must demonstrate transparency.
- Needs accurate time tracking.
- Must communicate progress and expenses.
- The client may question whether hours spent were necessary.
- There may be pressure to prove productivity.
- Resource utilization and billing must be managed carefully.

## 7. Fixed Scope vs. Time and Materials

The two commercial models differ in how they handle cost, requirements, flexibility, financial risk, and project management.

| Factor | Fixed Scope | Time and Materials |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Usually predetermined | Based on actual time and resources |
| Requirements | Need to be relatively clear | Can evolve |
| Scope flexibility | Limited without formal changes | More flexible |
| Budget predictability | Higher | Lower |
| Timeline predictability | Generally easier to estimate if scope is stable | Depends on evolving work |
| Change management | Formal change requests often necessary | Changes can be prioritized more easily |
| Financial risk | Service provider carries more estimation risk | Client carries more cost risk |
| Best suited for | Well-defined projects | Uncertain or evolving projects |
| Client involvement | Approvals and milestone reviews | Ongoing prioritization and feedback |
| Project Manager's focus | Scope, schedule, cost, quality, and change control | Budget, utilization, priorities, transparency, and delivery |

### 7.1 When Should You Choose Fixed Scope?

Fixed Scope is generally suitable when:

- Requirements are clearly defined.
- Deliverables are well understood.
- The project has limited uncertainty.
- The client has a fixed budget.
- The expected outcome can be agreed upon in advance.

### 7.2 When Should You Choose T&M?

T&M is generally suitable when:

- Requirements are uncertain.
- The project is expected to evolve.
- The client wants flexibility.
- New features may be introduced frequently.
- The client wants to prioritize work continuously.
- The project involves exploration or ongoing development.

## 8. Retainers

### 8.1 What Is a Retainer?

A retainer is an ongoing commercial agreement in which a client pays a service provider a recurring fee to reserve capacity, provide continuous services, or maintain access to specific expertise over a defined period.

A retainer does not necessarily mean that the client can request unlimited work.

The agreement should specify:

- What services are included.
- The capacity or service level provided.
- The duration of the agreement.
- The responsibilities of both parties.
- What services are excluded.
- How additional work is billed.
- Whether unused capacity can be carried forward.

Retainers are commonly used for:

- Ongoing software development
- Maintenance and support
- Technical consulting
- Dedicated development resources
- Website maintenance
- Infrastructure management

### 8.2 Types of Retainers

A retainer can be structured in different ways.

#### A. Hour-Based Retainer

The client pays for a certain number of hours each month.

The client is essentially purchasing access to a defined amount of service capacity.

**Example:**

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Monthly payment | BDT 100,000 |
| Included development capacity | 40 hours |
| Additional work | BDT 2,500 per hour |
| Unused hours | Expire at the end of the month unless rollover is agreed |

In this model, the client pays a recurring amount for a predefined number of working hours.

If the client needs additional work beyond the included hours, the extra hours are billed separately according to the agreement.

#### B. Service-Based Retainer

The client pays for a defined set of ongoing services rather than a specific number of hours.

The client does not necessarily need to know how many hours were spent on every task, as long as the provider fulfills the agreement.

**Example:**

A client pays BDT 50,000 per month for:

- Website maintenance
- Security updates
- Bug fixes
- Performance monitoring
- Monthly reporting
- Technical support

The service provider is responsible for delivering the agreed services throughout the contract period.

#### C. Dedicated Resource Retainer

The client pays for a dedicated or partially dedicated resource.

This model is common in outsourcing and staff augmentation arrangements.

The client essentially pays to reserve a specific professional's working capacity for an agreed period.

**Example:**

The client pays BDT 180,000 per month for a dedicated frontend developer who works on the client's system during agreed working hours.

The developer works as part of the client's development team while remaining employed or managed by the service provider.

---

## 9. Retainer vs. Time and Materials

Although both models are used in service-based companies, they have different payment and capacity structures.

| Factor | Time and Materials | Retainer |
|---|---|---|
| Payment | Based on actual time and resources used | Recurring agreed fee |
| Billing | Usually based on work performed during a billing period | Usually fixed monthly or periodic payment |
| Capacity | May be allocated as work is requested | Often reserves agreed capacity or access |
| Workload | Can vary based on client demand | May have agreed service limits |
| Cost | May fluctuate | More predictable recurring payment |
| Purpose | Pay for work performed | Maintain ongoing service or access to capacity |
| Typical use | Development projects with evolving requirements | Support, maintenance, ongoing development, consulting |

### 9.1 Can a Retainer and T&M Be Combined?

Yes. A retainer may also be structured using T&M principles.

For example, a client may pay a monthly retainer for 40 hours of development, with additional hours billed separately.

In this arrangement:

- The monthly retainer reserves 40 hours of development capacity.
- The client pays a fixed recurring amount.
- The service provider delivers work within the agreed capacity.
- Additional hours are billed according to the agreed hourly rate.
- Unused hours are handled according to the contract.

This is a combination of a recurring retainer agreement and a Time and Materials billing structure.
