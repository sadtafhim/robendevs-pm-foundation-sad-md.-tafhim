# Project Documentation, Communication, and Commercial Models

These topics explain how software projects are documented, how teams communicate, and how client agreements affect project delivery.

## 1. Project Artefacts

Project artefacts are documents and records used throughout a project to keep requirements, tasks, designs, and testing organised.

### Main Project Artefacts

- **PRD (Product Requirements Document):** Explains what needs to be built, why it is needed, and how the product should behave. It helps developers and designers understand the requirements.
- **Design Files:** Contain UI layouts, wireframes, prototypes, and user flows. They show how the product should look and work.
- **Backlog:** A prioritised list of user stories, tasks, bugs, and improvements that need to be completed.
- **Test Plan:** Defines what needs to be tested, the testing approach, scope, and responsibilities.
- **Test Cases:** Provide specific testing steps, expected results, and actual results to verify functionality.
- **Release Notes:** Explain what has changed in a release, including new features, improvements, bug fixes, and known issues.

### Traceability

Traceability means connecting requirements to development tasks and test cases.

It helps the team:
- Ensure every requirement is implemented.
- Confirm that important requirements are tested.
- Identify missing work and reduce misunderstandings.

## 2. Communication Rhythm

Regular communication keeps clients and internal teams informed about project progress and potential problems.

- **Client Calls:** Used to discuss requirements, demonstrate progress, collect feedback, resolve concerns, and confirm decisions.
- **Internal Standups:** Short team meetings where members share what they completed, what they will work on next, and any blockers.
- **Weekly Status Reports:** Written updates covering completed work, current progress, upcoming tasks, risks, blockers, and decisions needed from the client.

The goal is to maintain transparency and identify problems before they affect delivery.

## 3. Service Work vs Product Work

- **Service Work:** Software is developed or maintained for a specific client based on their requirements. The solution is usually tailored to that client's needs.
- **Product Work:** A software product is developed for multiple customers. The focus is on reusable features, product improvements, and long-term development.

**Main Difference:** Service work focuses on delivering solutions to individual clients, while product work focuses on building and improving a solution for a wider market.

## 4. Client Projects

A client project involves delivering software according to an agreed set of requirements, budget, and timeline.

The team needs to:
- Understand the client's business goals and requirements.
- Define project scope and deliverables.
- Agree on timelines, responsibilities, and expectations.
- Communicate progress and manage feedback.
- Handle changes through an agreed approval process.

Clear agreements and regular communication help prevent misunderstandings.

## 5. Commercial Models

Commercial models define how a client pays for software development services.

### Fixed Scope

The project requirements, deliverables, cost, and timeline are agreed upon before development begins.

- Suitable when requirements are clear and stable.
- Major changes may require additional cost and time.
- The team must manage scope carefully.

### Time and Materials (T&M)

The client pays for the actual time and resources used during development.

- Suitable when requirements may change.
- Allows flexibility to adjust priorities.
- Costs depend on the amount of work completed.

### Retainer

The client pays a recurring fee to receive ongoing development, maintenance, or support services.

- Provides continuous access to the development team.
- Useful for long-term support and regular improvements.
- Work is usually managed within an agreed period or capacity.

## 6. Scope Creep and Change Management

**Scope creep** happens when additional features or requirements are added beyond the original agreement without properly adjusting the budget, timeline, or resources.

It can cause delays, increased costs, and pressure on the development team.

### Managing Scope Changes

1. **Document the Request:** Clearly record what the client wants to change.
2. **Assess the Impact:** Evaluate how it affects cost, timeline, resources, and existing work.
3. **Discuss with the Client:** Explain the impact and possible options.
4. **Get Approval:** Agree on any changes to scope, budget, or timeline.
5. **Update Documentation:** Revise the requirements, backlog, and project plan.

Not every new request should be added immediately. Changes should be evaluated and approved before the team commits to them.
